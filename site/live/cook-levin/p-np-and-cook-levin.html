Chapter 11

P, NP and Cook-Levin

Complexity theory. A review; it needs “P and NP”, and you can skip it if you know Cook-Levin. We state the theorem this whole book riffs on, and say exactly what it means for a machine with a two-bit register.

“P and NP” defines the two classes, and “Nibble, sized” places the toy among them. This chapter states the theorem that ties NP to one formal language, SAT, and says what it means for the toy.

SAT, as hard as all of NP §

A reduction from a formal language A to a formal language B is a polynomial-time translation of A's inputs into B's that keeps every answer: x is in A exactly when its translation is in B. A machine for B then answers A too. A formal language is NP-complete when it is in NP and every language in NP reduces to it. In 1971 Stephen Cook, in "The Complexity of Theorem-Proving Procedures", and independently Leonid Levin, published in 1973, showed that SAT is as hard as anything in NP. Their proof takes the checking machine, which runs for a polynomial number of steps, and writes each step of its run as clauses: the whole run becomes one formula, satisfiable exactly when a certificate exists.

Guessing, and the computation history §

Michael Sipser's MIT lecture on the theorem (lecture 16) frames NP with a nondeterministic machine. At each choice it forks and follows every branch at once, and it accepts if any branch accepts. Such a machine forks for free and follows every branch, which is different from guessing at random. For the toy, the choices are the program's twelve bits. A nondeterministic machine guesses them two at a time, then runs the guessed program on the four inputs. A player clicking through a puzzle's buttons, knowing only the buttons, is that machine run one branch at a time, as “Which instructions are needed” counts.

Sipser calls the record of one branch's run, every state from the start to the verdict, its computation history. Cook and Levin write a formula that is satisfiable exactly when some branch has an accepting computation history. For the toy, a history is the tableau of “The Cook-Levin machine”: seven rows by four cores.

The toy as a checker §

The toy is such a checker. Given a problem and a program, it runs the program on four inputs and says whether the program decides the problem; the program is the certificate. The rest of the book takes that checker apart two ways: Cook and Levin's route, from gates to a tableau to clauses for a SAT solver (“The Boolean view” on), and the riff's own, its step as polynomials composed six times (“The program left unknown” on).

Either way, the checker is the whole of it: twelve bits of certificate, four inputs and one verdict.
