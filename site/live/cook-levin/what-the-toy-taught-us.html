A riff on Cook-Levin Chapters

Chapter 42

What the toy taught us

Every frame. A review; it stands alone. Here is what Cook-Levin looks like from inside a machine with a two-bit register, in one page, each paragraph pointing back to the chapters it gathers.

A checker is a circuit unrolled in time. The toy's checker runs a program on four inputs and compares the verdicts with a problem. Wired as hardware it is 24 copies of one 59-gate stepper and four comparators (“The circuit, in full”); drawn as Cook's tableau it is seven rows by four cores (“The tableau, filled in”). The size of the grid is the running time times the width, which is why a fast checker gives a small formula.

Leave the certificate free and the checker becomes a question. Fix the problem, leave the program's twelve bits unknown, and require the output: the circuit now asks which programs fit (“The Cook-Levin machine”). As clauses that is 416 variables and 1,216 clauses a puzzle, and its satisfying assignments are exactly the programs that decide it, by propagation alone (Theorem 10). That is the theorem, at the size of a puzzle page.

Naming beats expanding. Tseitin's encoding gives each gate a variable and grows one stepper's clauses a step. Multiplying the same logic out grows by a factor a step: 3,869 clauses for the decider by distribution (“From equations to CNF”), 10,324 terms for accepted as a polynomial even with x² = x (“Six steps composed”), and about 10^54 terms in the general ring, where x² stays x² (“Arithmetization”). Cook and Levin's formula stays polynomial because it names.

A yes is short; a refutation can be long. A yes comes with a certificate you check by running it: twelve bits for {0, 2, 3}, 56 bits for is_not_14 at 28 lines. A refutation takes 9,012 bytes to show that five lines are too few for {0, 2, 3}, and 17,192,870 bytes that 27 lines are too few for is_not_14 (“is_not_14 needs 28 lines”). That asymmetry is the one NP is built on (“P and NP”).

Brute force first, and it wins while everything fits in a list. Running all 4,096 programs settles the toy, and one search of 374,003 states settles all 65,536 problems of Nibble++ in 0.67 s, where rocsat took 17.0 s to refute one length of one of them (“Guided search”). SAT earns its keep when the states outgrow any list and the circuit stays small; a theorem, where one exists, beats both.

Complexity lives in growth. For a fixed machine every question is a table lookup. P and NP have something to say only once the register and the program are given sizes that grow (“Nibble, sized”), and that is where the open questions are (“What comes next”).

And every road agrees. The interpreter, the gates, the circuit, the clauses, the solver and the polynomials give the same answer at every point they share: the book's thesis, checked at all 16,384 points (“The oracles”).
