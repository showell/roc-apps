Chapter 24

Naming the gates

Tseitin's encoding turns a circuit into clauses, with one fresh variable for each gate. Here it turns a circuit of three gates into nine clauses, and then we watch a solver's simplest rule run the circuit forward.

A solver reads CNF, and the machine is a circuit, gates feeding gates. Writing a circuit as CNF directly multiplies its ANDs out over its ORs, and each layer of gates can multiply the size again, as f's text grew six-fold a line in “One formula for the whole machine”. Grigori Tseitin's encoding names instead: each gate's output gets a fresh variable, and a few clauses say that the variable equals its gate. The formula grows by three clauses a gate, however deep the circuit is. “Checking a claim” and “From equations to CNF” used it at full size; here it is small enough to watch, before “Cook-Levin as SAT” uses it on every puzzle.

Propagation runs the circuit §

Unit propagation is the rule of “The SAT solver”: a clause whose literals are all false but one forces that one. Fix the three inputs, and each gate's clauses force its output as soon as its inputs are known, in the circuit's own order, so propagation evaluates the circuit. sat/walkthrough.shale runs it, with a few lines of propagation of its own. A true case:

inputs fixed: a = true, b = true, c = false
g1 = true, from (g1 OR NOT a OR NOT b)
g2 = true, from (g2 OR c)
out = true, from (out OR NOT g1)
every clause has a true literal, (out) among them

These are the values of sat/readable/circuit_model.v, a model of the formula. A false case:

inputs fixed: a = false, b = true, c = true
g1 = false, from (NOT g1 OR a)
g2 = false, from (NOT g2 OR NOT c)
out = false, from (NOT out OR g1 OR g2)
conflict: every literal of (out) is false

The gates compute out = 0, and the claim asks for 1. With these inputs the formula is unsatisfiable, and seeing it took three forced values and zero guesses. The whole decider behaves the same way in “From equations to CNF”: with every input fixed, rocsat answers in 0 decisions, one propagation a variable.

Sipser's tableau and our circuit §

Sipser's proof of Cook-Levin (“P, NP and Cook-Levin”) gives each cell of the tableau its own variables, one for each symbol the cell might hold, and states the machine's rules by windows: every 2 × 3 block of cells must be one the rules allow. A window touches six cells, a fixed number, so its rule is a formula of constant size, and multiplying it out into CNF costs a constant a window, using the cells' variables alone. Our formula is one circuit, gates feeding gates down six steps, and multiplied out it grows by a factor a step: 3,869 clauses for the decider by distribution (“From equations to CNF”). So we name every gate, three clauses each. Both routes stay polynomial: Sipser's by keeping each piece small, ours by Tseitin's names.

Three gates, nine clauses, and the solver's one rule computes the circuit.
