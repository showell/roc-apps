The rules

The board is 4 × 4 tiles, all dark. The cube starts on a1, the north-west corner, with its painted face up.

The language is RollScript. A program is a list of rolls: N, E, S or W. Each roll tips the cube over an edge into the next tile. Each time the cube lands with the paint down, the tile under it flips, dark to lit or lit to dark. The program's meaning is the set of tiles lit at the end, wherever the cube stops.

A roll off the board halts the program. The cube stays where it was, the tiles lit so far are the program's set, and the rolls after the halt do nothing.

A program is at most 45 rolls long. That is just long enough to light any set of tiles but one: lighting every tile takes 47 rolls.

The lit set is a number

The tiles are numbered 0 to 15, row by row from the north-west corner. Each also has a chess name, its column a to d and its row 1 to 4:

0 a11 b12 c13 d1
4 a25 b26 c27 d2
8 a39 b310 c311 d3
12 a413 b414 c415 d4

Tile n lit is bit n of a 16-bit number, so a program's lit set, its result, is one of 65,536 numbers. b2 alone is tile 5, 25 = 32, binary 0000 0000 0010 0000; tile 0, a1, is the last binary digit.

So a program answers a question about the numbers 0 to 15: it lights tile n when the answer for n is yes. As you roll, the page names the question the lit set answers, the way the Nibble++ sandbox names the language a program decides, from the same table of names (cost 4 or less, the cheapest for each set). The program that lights 0, 5, 10 and 15 answers is_mult_of_5, and the program that lights every tile but 14 answers is_not_14. A set with no short name, such as most sets of scattered tiles, shows its number alone.

The even numbers are columns a and c, and the odd ones b and d, yet the odd ones cost two rolls more. is_not_14 was the hardest question for Nibble++. Here it costs 44, against the 45 for is_not_13: 14 is c4, on the other colour from a1.

Playing

Type a program, or roll the cube with the arrow buttons or the arrow keys, or click a tile next to the cube (or drag the cube onto it). Play, Back, Forward and Reset move through the run. A roll off the board can only be typed, or made with a button or key, since no tile is there to click.

The listing under the program is the program a line a roll, as a Nibble++ program is a line an instruction: the line's number, its opcode (a roll's two bits: N 00, E 01, S 10, W 11), the roll, where the paint points after it (U, D, N, S, E or W), and what it did: the tile it lit or darkened, or the halt. The current line is highlighted; a click on a line shows the run there. Under the text box is the program's machine code, its opcodes line 1 first and the number they make read as one binary number.

When the paint lands face down, the tile under the cube flashes and the cube dips, and the tile keeps a blue frame, its stamp, for the rest of the run. When the paint faces a side, the floor glows blue on that side of the cube, so a face turned away from you still shows. The compass in the canvas's corner says where the paint points: N, E, S or W, or U and D in its middle.

A set's par is the fewest rolls of any program that ends with exactly that set lit. The page shows your program's lit set, as tiles and as a number in decimal and binary, its par, its name, and your program's length.

The samples

ESWN loop
ESWN: a loop around a1, b1, b2 and a2. The cube is back on a1 with nothing lit, the paint facing north.
b2 alone
ESENSNWS lights b2 in its par, 8. It lights c2 and puts it out again on the way.
the diagonal
ESSENNWWESESESNNWW lights a1, b2, c3 and d4, tiles 0, 5, 10 and 15, in their par, 18: it answers is_mult_of_5.
a halt
ESENNSSS: the fifth roll, N from c1, leaves the board, so the program halts with c2, tile 6, lit. The three S rolls after it do nothing.
the longest
EEESWNESWNESSWWENWENWSWNESWESWESWNESNESENWNES lights every tile but d4 in its par, 45, the longest program there is.
